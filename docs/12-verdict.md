# Verdict

## Is omega-3 supplementation worthwhile?

**Conditionally yes — for a narrower set of reasons than the market claims, and
at a much lower cost than most people pay.**

The evidence supports three uses with real outcome data:

1. **Lowering triglycerides at 2-4 g/day.** This is the strongest, most
   reproducible effect: 20-30% at 4 g/day. For severe hypertriglyceridaemia it is
   a medical treatment, ideally with a prescription product.
2. **A modest reduction in myocardial infarction at about 1 g/day in primary
   prevention.** VITAL's HR 0.72 for total MI is real, even though the primary
   composite was null. The absolute benefit is small.
3. **Pregnancy, for preterm birth risk.** The pooled reduction is large; the
   largest single trial was null. This is a clinical discussion, not a universal
   recommendation.

Everything else is weak, unproven, or disproven. Omega-3 does **not** prevent
death, dementia, cancer, dry eye, ADHD symptoms, or macular degeneration, and it
does not meaningfully build muscle or treat depression as monotherapy.

## Who benefits

| Group | Recommendation | Dose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Adults with triglycerides 200-499 mg/dL | Discuss with a clinician; omega-3 is an option alongside diet, statins and other agents | 2-4 g/day |
| Adults with triglycerides ≥500 mg/dL | Medical management; prescription icosapent ethyl or omega-3-acid ethyl esters | 4 g/day, supervised |
| Recent myocardial infarction | Guideline-consistent ~1 g/day is reasonable | ~1 g/day |
| People who eat little or no fish | 1 g/day is a low-risk, low-cost way to cover a likely deficit | 0.25-1 g/day |
| Pregnant women | Discuss DHA supplementation, especially with low fish intake | 500-1,000 mg, ≥500 mg DHA |
| Vegans and people with fish allergy | Algal oil is the appropriate source | 0.5-1 g/day |
| Some people with rheumatoid arthritis | Possible small adjunct at high dose; low certainty | 2.7-3 g/day |
| Athletes subject to drug testing | Only if NSF Certified for Sport; no ergogenic claim | 1-2 g/day |

## Who does not benefit

- **Healthy adults hoping to prevent dementia or cognitive decline.** The
  high-certainty answer is no.
- **Children with ADHD.** High-certainty null.
- **People with dry eye.** The largest trial was null.
- **People trying to prevent cancer.** VITAL found no reduction.
- **People with osteoarthritis expecting pain relief.** Large trials were null.
- **Anyone taking 1 g/day and expecting REDUCE-IT's 25% risk reduction.** That
  result belongs to a 4 g/day prescription drug.
- **People who already eat oily fish twice a week.** The marginal benefit is
  close to zero; food first.

## Who should avoid specific formulations

| Situation | Avoid | Use instead |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High LDL-C or familial hypercholesterolaemia | DHA-dominant products | EPA-dominant (e.g. OmegaVia) |
| Prior atrial fibrillation or high AF risk | Doses >1 g/day | ≤1 g/day, or none |
| Pregnancy | Cod liver oil (vitamin A) | Low-vitamin-A fish oil or algal DHA |
| Also taking a multivitamin with vitamin A | Cod liver oil | Fish body oil |
| Liver disease or vitamin A excess | Cod liver oil | Fish body oil |
| Fish allergy | Fish oil, fish-gelatin capsules | Algal oil |
| Warfarin or multiple antithrombotics | Unmonitored high doses | Monitor INR; discuss |
| Upcoming surgery | Not necessarily anything — evidence does not require stopping | Individualise |
| Severe hypertriglyceridaemia | Self-dosing with supplements | Clinician-supervised prescription therapy |

## Which products offer the best quality and value

Value is **cost per gram of EPA+DHA**, adjusted for concentration, certification
and disclosure. On that basis:

**Best overall value:** **Viva Naturals Triple Strength** ($0.30/g, rTG, 2,070 mg
per serving, IFOS) and **Sports Research Triple Strength** ($0.31/g, rTG, IFOS
5-star, MSC). Both are high-concentration, third-party tested, and roughly
one-seventh the cost per gram of krill oil.

**Cheapest per gram:** **InnovixLabs Triple Strength** ($0.30/g, 200-count,
IFOS 5-star), an ethyl ester. It is the clearest example of why form premiums are
hard to justify.

**Best EPA-dominant:** **OmegaVia Ultra** ($0.64/g, 780 mg EPA, IFOS 5-star) for
people who need to avoid a DHA-driven LDL rise.

**Best budget staple:** **NOW Ultra Omega-3** ($0.41/g, 750 mg per softgel,
widely available).

**Best vegan:** **Deva Vegan Omega-3** ($1.18/g guaranteed minimum) or **Nordic
Naturals Algae Omega** ($1.76/g, DHA-dominant, third-party tested). Algal oil is
inherently more expensive per gram than fish oil; that is a real trade-off, not a
markup.

**Cod liver oil:** **Carlson** ($1.05/g, IFOS) is well tested, but one teaspoon
supplies 8.5% of the adult vitamin A upper limit per teaspoon, typical cod liver oils about 45%, and high-vitamin versions can exceed it. Treat it as a vitamin product.

**Poor value:** **MegaRed krill** ($4.59/g), **iWi algal** ($4.53/g) and
**Ovega-3** ($2.25/g). They are not fake; they are simply expensive per gram of
the nutrient that has evidence behind it.

## What would change this verdict

- A large trial showing that 1-2 g/day reduces dementia or cognitive decline.
- A head-to-head trial showing rTG produces better clinical outcomes than EE at
  equal doses.
- Resolution of the REDUCE-IT versus STRENGTH contradiction, which would clarify
  whether high-dose EPA+DHA has any role beyond triglycerides.
- Long-term data on atrial fibrillation at 1-2 g/day in healthy adults.
- A validated, affordable omega-3 index test that changes management.

None of these exists today. The verdict should be revisited if they do.

## The bottom line in five sentences

1. Omega-3 supplements lower triglycerides reliably at 4 g/day and probably
   reduce myocardial infarction slightly at 1 g/day; they do not extend life or
   prevent dementia, cancer, dry eye or ADHD.
2. Most people take 600 mg/day, which is below every dose that produced a
   cardiovascular benefit.
3. Ethyl esters and triglycerides differ modestly in absorption, and the
   difference largely disappears with a fatty meal; rTG is not worth a large
   premium.
4. Krill and algal oils are legitimate sources but cost 2-15 times more per gram
   of EPA+DHA, and krill's "potency" claims are false.
5. Buy the cheapest high-concentration product with a lot-level freshness test,
   take it with food, do not escalate the dose for benefits that do not exist,
   and avoid cod liver oil if you are pregnant or already taking vitamin A.
