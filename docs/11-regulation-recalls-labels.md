# Regulation, recalls and misleading labels

## Two regulatory universes

The same molecule can be a drug or a supplement, with completely different
evidence requirements.

| | Prescription omega-3 | Dietary supplement |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal basis (US) | New Drug Application (NDA) | DSHEA 1994 |
| Pre-market approval | Yes: safety, efficacy, manufacturing | **No** |
| Purity specification | Defined active substance, e.g. ≥96% icosapent ethyl | Meets label claim; GMP |
| Dose | Fixed, e.g. 4 g/day | Variable |
| Disease claims | Allowed within the approved indication | **Not allowed** |
| Example | Vascepa (icosapent ethyl), Lovaza | Fish oil softgels |

**Vascepa** is icosapent ethyl: a single-molecule ethyl ester of EPA, ≥96% pure,
approved at 4 g/day for cardiovascular risk reduction on the basis of REDUCE-IT
([R116](references.md), [R117](references.md)). The EMA's assessment explicitly
notes that EPA and DHA have different pharmacological effects and that
"omega-3-acid ethyl esters 90" is not equivalent to icosapent ethyl.

A fish oil supplement is therefore **not** a substitute for icosapent ethyl, and
a supplement cannot legally claim to reduce heart attacks. When a brand implies
otherwise, it is making an unapproved drug claim.

## What happened to Epanova

Epanova (omega-3 carboxylic acids) was an FDA-approved prescription product for
severe hypertriglyceridaemia. Its outcomes trial, STRENGTH, was stopped in
January 2020 for futility and found no cardiovascular benefit (HR 0.99)
([R03](references.md)). Marketing was discontinued, and the approval was
withdrawn at the company's request in 2021 ([R118](references.md)).

This is worth stating precisely: Epanova was **not withdrawn for safety**. It
failed to show benefit. It is nonetheless a useful case study in how a plausible
mechanism (lower triglycerides) does not guarantee a clinical outcome.

## Enforcement actions

The FDA has issued warning letters for omega-3 products that crossed from
structure/function claims into disease claims:

| Year | Company | Issue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 2017 | Maine Natural Health | CGMP and labelling violations, including a fish oil product |
| 2017 | Wellness Resources | DHA products with disease claims |
| 2020 | Bodyhealth.com | "Omega 3 Health" disease claims |
| 2021 | 1st Phorm | Fish oil claims about blood pressure and triglycerides made it an unapproved new drug ([R113](references.md)) |
| 2023 | Source-Omega | Algae omega-3 with Alzheimer's claims ([R114](references.md)) |

The pattern is consistent: **blood pressure, triglycerides, Alzheimer's and
"reduces heart attack risk"** are the claims most likely to trigger enforcement,
because they are disease claims.

## Litigation

Several US class actions have targeted omega-3 labelling, with mixed results:

- **Ethyl ester identity:** suits against Dr. Tobias and other brands allege that
  products sold as "fish oil" are chemically synthesised ethyl esters. A motion
  to dismiss was granted in part and denied in part in 2024, so the theory has
  survived initial pleading but has not been finally adjudicated.
- **Costco/Kirkland label claim:** a suit alleging EPA/DHA shortfall was met with
  two independent laboratory reports confirming label conformance.
- **Walmart "heart health" claims:** dismissed with prejudice in 2025; the court
  noted that allegations about oxidised oil were not tied to the products at
  issue.

**What this means:** labelling litigation is common, and a lawsuit is not proof
of a defect. The verifiable problems are the ones documented by testing, not the
ones alleged in complaints.

## Recalls

There is no widely documented Class I recall of a mainstream fish oil supplement
for contamination or potency in the period reviewed. Recalls that do occur in
this category are typically for **unapproved drug claims**, **CGMP violations**,
or **allergen/labelling errors**, not for mercury or dioxins. Any claim that "fish
oil supplements are being recalled" should be checked against the FDA recall
database before it is repeated.

## Label rules that matter

### United States

- A supplement labelled with a specific EPA/DHA amount that contains **less than
  80%** of it is considered misbranded ([R27](references.md)).
- Structure/function claims are not pre-approved, must be truthful, and must
  carry the disclaimer "not intended to diagnose, treat, cure, or prevent any
  disease" ([R124](references.md)).
- The qualified health claim for EPA/DHA and coronary heart disease says:
  *"Supportive but not conclusive research shows that consumption of EPA and DHA
  omega-3 fatty acids may reduce the risk of coronary heart disease."* It applies
  at **≤3 g/day total EPA+DHA**, and supplement labels should not recommend more
  than **2 g/day** ([R47](references.md)).
- In 2019 the FDA concluded that **≤5 g/day** of EPA+DHA does not increase
  bleeding risk and is safe and lawful; above that, the evidence is insufficient
  ([R48](references.md)). This is a claim condition, **not a legal maximum**.

### European Union

Authorised claims and their conditions ([R115](references.md)):

| Claim | Condition |
| --- | --- |
| EPA and DHA contribute to normal heart function | 250 mg/day EPA+DHA |
| DHA contributes to maintenance of normal brain function | 250 mg/day DHA |
| DHA contributes to maintenance of normal vision | 250 mg/day DHA |
| DHA (with EPA) contributes to normal blood triglyceride levels | 2 g/day DHA or EPA+DHA; warning not to exceed 5 g/day; not for children |
| EPA and DHA contribute to normal blood pressure | 3 g/day EPA+DHA |

Note what these claims are: **maintenance of normal function**, at low doses.
They are not treatment claims, and they do not imply the outcome-trial benefits
discussed in [the evidence review](04-evidence-review.md).

## The common misleading-label tactics

1. **"1,200 mg fish oil"** with no EPA/DHA split — the number is oil mass, not
   the active dose.
2. **"Total omega-3 700 mg"** — includes ALA/DPA and may overstate EPA+DHA.
3. **Serving size of two or three softgels** — the front-of-pack number is often
   per serving, not per capsule.
4. **"Krill oil 50× more potent"** — false; krill is 12-19% EPA+DHA.
5. **"Pharmaceutical grade"** — no legal definition.
6. **"Molecularly distilled"** — a process, not a test result; it does not fix
   oxidation.
7. **"Supports heart health"** — a structure/function claim, not evidence of
   reduced events.
8. **Borrowing drug data** — implying REDUCE-IT results for a 1 g/day supplement.
9. **"Mercury-free"** — true of most refined oils but used to imply all
   contaminants are absent.
10. **Certification logos without lot reports** — a brand-level programme is not
    a bottle-level test.

## What a consumer can actually verify

- The **Supplement Facts panel**, per serving, with EPA and DHA in milligrams.
- The **serving size** and **servings per container**.
- A **lot-specific IFOS report** or COA, if the brand publishes one.
- The **expiry date** and storage instructions.
- The **certification scope** (sourcing vs quality vs sport).
- The **price per gram of EPA+DHA**, which no brand prints but anyone can
  calculate.
