# EPA and DHA: what they are and what they do

## The molecules

EPA (eicosapentaenoic acid, 20:5 n-3) and DHA (docosahexaenoic acid, 22:6 n-3)
are long-chain omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids. "Omega-3" means the first
double bond sits three carbons from the methyl end; the rest of the structure
determines what the molecule can do.

They are **conditionally essential**: humans can synthesise them from
alpha-linolenic acid (ALA, 18:3 n-3), but the conversion is inefficient and
competitively inhibited by linoleic acid (omega-6). Typical conversion of ALA to
EPA is a few percent, and to DHA lower still, with substantial individual
variation. This is why "contains flaxseed oil" is not an omega-3 claim in the
sense that matters clinically.

| Property | EPA | DHA |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Carbon chain | 20 | 22 |
| Double bonds | 5 | 6 |
| Main tissue | Cell membranes, eicosanoid precursor pool | Retina, brain grey matter, sperm, cardiac membranes |
| Typical supplement role | Triglyceride lowering, anti-inflammatory eicosanoids | Membrane structure, visual and neural development |
| Effect on LDL-C | Neutral to slightly favourable | Raises LDL-C modestly |
| Effect on triglycerides | Lowers | Lowers |
| Distinctive evidence | REDUCE-IT (as icosapent ethyl), depression signal | Infant development, retinal function |

## Why the distinction matters commercially

"Omega-3" is used as if it were one nutrient. It is not, and the difference is
exploited in both directions:

- **EPA-only** products can claim the REDUCE-IT result, but REDUCE-IT tested a
  specific purified ethyl ester drug at 4 g/day, not a supplement.
- **DHA-rich** products can claim brain and eye benefits, which are authorised EU
  claims only at 250 mg DHA/day and are about *maintenance of normal function*,
  not treatment or enhancement.
- **Krill and algal oils** often advertise "omega-3" while delivering 90-250 mg
  of EPA+DHA per capsule — a fraction of a concentrated fish oil.

## Biological roles

### Membranes and fluidity

DHA is the most abundant omega-3 in retinal photoreceptors and neuronal
membranes. It is concentrated in phosphatidylethanolamine and
phosphatidylserine, where its six double bonds produce a highly flexible acyl
chain. That flexibility affects receptor signalling, membrane protein function
and the production of neuroprotectin D1. This is the mechanistic basis for the
brain and eye claims, and it is genuine physiology. It does **not** follow that
supplementing an already sufficient person improves cognition — the trials say it
does not (see [the evidence review](04-evidence-review.md)).

### Eicosanoids and inflammation

EPA competes with arachidonic acid for cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase enzymes,
shifting production toward less inflammatory mediators (resolvins, protectins,
maresins). This is a real biochemical effect: CRP falls by about 0.4 SMD in
pooled trials ([OTH-03](generated/evidence-tables.md)). Whether that translates
into clinical benefit depends entirely on the disease, and for most diseases the
answer so far is "less than hoped".

### Lipoproteins and triglycerides

EPA and DHA reduce hepatic triglyceride synthesis and secretion, increase
fatty-acid beta-oxidation, and change lipoprotein lipase activity. At 4 g/day,
triglycerides fall 20-30% — the largest and most reproducible clinical effect of
omega-3 supplementation ([LIP-01](generated/evidence-tables.md)). DHA, but not
EPA, raises LDL-C by roughly 3-7 mg/dL, a distinction that matters when choosing
a product for someone with high LDL.

### Membranes, platelets and bleeding

High-dose omega-3 displaces arachidonic acid from platelet membranes, modestly
reducing thromboxane A2 production and platelet aggregation. This is measurable
but clinically small: pooled bleeding risk across 120,643 patients is RR 1.09
(0.91-1.31), and the OPERA trial found *fewer* transfusions in cardiac surgery
patients given 8-10 g/day pre-operatively ([SAF-01](generated/evidence-tables.md),
[SAF-04](generated/evidence-tables.md)).

### The omega-3 index

The omega-3 index is EPA+DHA as a percentage of total fatty acids in red blood
cell membranes, reflecting roughly the last four months of intake. In the
original framing, **≥8% is associated with the lowest coronary death risk and
≤4% with the highest**; typical Western values are 3-5% ([R21](references.md),
[R22](references.md)). A 1-SD higher index was associated with HR 0.85 (0.80-0.91)
for fatal CHD across 10 cohorts. This is strong observational association, not
proof that raising the index with capsules reduces risk.

## What EPA and DHA do not do

- They are not a substitute for statins, antihypertensives, or dietary change.
- They do not reverse atherosclerosis.
- They do not cross the blood-brain barrier in quantities that have been shown to
  improve cognition in healthy adults.
- They do not "balance" omega-6 in a way that has been proven to change hard
  outcomes; the omega-6:omega-3 ratio is a hypothesis, not a validated target.

## Practical implication

The biology supports two narrow, well-evidenced uses: **triglyceride lowering at
pharmacological doses**, and **correcting a genuine deficiency** (low omega-3
index, low fish intake, pregnancy, parenteral nutrition). Everything else sits
somewhere between plausible and disproven, and is graded as such in the
[evidence tables](generated/evidence-tables.md).
