# Sources, formulations and bioavailability

## The four commercial sources

| Source | What it is | Typical EPA+DHA concentration | Vitamin content | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fish body oil | Rendered from anchovy, sardine, mackerel, herring, pollock; often a by-product of fishmeal | 18-30% unrefined; 50-85% after concentration | None added | The basis of nearly all outcome trials |
| Cod liver oil | Oil from cod livers; traditional | 10-28% (Ph. Eur. range) | **Vitamin A and D, naturally** | Old trials; modern use is as much a vitamin supplement |
| Krill oil | Extract of Antarctic krill, phospholipid-bound | 12-19% | Astaxanthin | Small trials; no outcome data |
| Algal oil | Fermented *Schizochytrium* or *Nannochloropsis* | 30-75% | None | Bioequivalence and TG trials |

## Triglyceride vs ethyl ester vs re-esterified triglyceride

This is the most consequential formulation question, and the marketing around it
is worse than the science.

**How the forms arise.** Natural fish oil is triglyceride (TG): three fatty acids
esterified to glycerol. To concentrate EPA and DHA, manufacturers transesterify
the oil into **ethyl esters (EE)**, distill off the short-chain and saturated
fatty acids, then either sell the EE or re-esterify the concentrate back onto
glycerol to make **re-esterified triglyceride (rTG)**.

**What the trials show.** The best-controlled head-to-head comparison gave 72
volunteers about 3.3 g EPA+DHA/day for two weeks and measured the rise across
serum lipid fractions ([R11](references.md)):

| Form | Relative bioavailability | Dose-adjusted |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Natural fish oil (TG) | 100% (reference) | 100% |
| Ethyl ester | **73%** | 76% |
| Free fatty acid | 91% | 86% |
| Re-esterified TG | **124%** | 134% |

Two important qualifications, both routinely omitted from marketing:

1. **The EE penalty is largely a meal-fat effect.** In the classic studies, EE
   absorption without a fatty meal was only about 20% of the free-acid reference;
   with a 44 g fat meal it roughly tripled, to about 60% ([R13](references.md),
   [R12](references.md)). Taking EE capsules with a meal containing fat largely
   closes the gap.
2. **Dyerberg's study was two weeks long, in 72 people, and the EE-vs-cod-liver-oil
   difference was not statistically significant.** It is a good pharmacokinetic
   study, not a clinical-outcome trial. No trial has shown that rTG produces
   better health outcomes than EE.

**The honest summary:** rTG is probably modestly better absorbed per milligram,
and EE is cheaper to produce and therefore usually cheaper per gram. Whether the
bioavailability difference survives a normal meal, and whether it matters for
outcomes, is unproven. Buy rTG if the price per gram of EPA+DHA is competitive;
do not pay a large premium for it, and do not believe "70% more absorbable".

## Krill oil

Krill oil's sales pitch is that its EPA and DHA are bound to phospholipids
(mostly phosphatidylcholine), which are absorbed differently from triglycerides,
and that it contains astaxanthin as an antioxidant.

**What is true:** krill oil contains phospholipid-bound omega-3, and
phospholipid absorption does differ. Krill oil is also a legitimate source of
EPA+DHA.

**What is not established:** that this translates into meaningfully better
delivery or clinical effect. In a 7-week trial, 3 g/day krill oil (543 mg
EPA+DHA) produced similar plasma EPA/DHA increases to 1.8 g/day fish oil (864 mg
EPA+DHA) — but krill delivered only 63% of the dose, so this is not evidence of
superiority ([R14](references.md)). A 64-trial network meta-analysis found **no
difference between krill and fish oil** for triglycerides, LDL-C or HDL-C once
dose was accounted for; the lipid effect was dose-driven ([R17](references.md)).

The practical problem is concentration. Krill oil is about 12-19% EPA+DHA.
A 500 mg krill softgel typically contains **90-95 mg EPA+DHA** — one-tenth of a
concentrated fish oil capsule. That is why krill costs **$2.80-$4.60 per gram** of
EPA+DHA in this review, versus $0.30-$0.65 for concentrated fish oil. You are
paying for phospholipids, astaxanthin and a smaller capsule, not for more
omega-3.

## Algal oil

Algal oil is produced by fermenting microalgae, so it is vegan, free of fish
allergens, and does not depend on wild fisheries.

**Evidence.** Algal DHA is bioequivalent to DHA from cooked salmon in a controlled
trial ([R19](references.md)). A 14-week trial in hypertriglyceridaemic adults
found algal EPA+DHA 2.4 g/day lowered triglycerides by 18.9%, versus 22.9% for
fish oil 2.0 g/day — comparable, with the algal arm receiving a higher dose
([R18](references.md)). Algal oil is a legitimate substitute, particularly for
DHA.

**Caveat.** Concentration and price vary enormously. In this sample, algal
products range from $1.18 to $4.53 per gram of EPA+DHA, and one 30-count bottle
delivers only 7.5 g of EPA+DHA in total.

## Cod liver oil

Cod liver oil is fish oil plus the liver's fat-soluble vitamin stores. That is
the whole issue.

A typical 5 mL teaspoon of a leading brand supplies about **370 mg EPA + 500 mg
DHA** — a moderate dose — plus **255 mcg RAE of vitamin A** and 10 mcg (400 IU)
of vitamin D ([R57](references.md)). The vitamin A is **8.5% of the adult upper limit** (3,000 mcg RAE/day) in a
single teaspoon for this product — but typical cod liver oils supply about
1,350 mcg RAE per teaspoon, roughly **45% of the upper limit**, and
"high-vitamin" cod liver oils can exceed it ([R59](references.md)).

Documented hypervitaminosis A from cod liver oil exists, including a
haemodialysis patient who took up to nine cod liver oil tablets daily for one to
two months and presented with hypercalcaemia and serum retinol 220 µg/dL
([R56](references.md)). Cod liver oil is therefore best understood as a
**combined vitamin A, vitamin D and omega-3 product** with a narrow safe dose
range, not as a generic fish oil.

## Free fatty acids and "sn-2" claims

Free fatty acid omega-3 is absorbed efficiently and does not carry the EE
meal-fat penalty, but it is irritating and rarely sold as a consumer supplement.
The "sn-2 DHA" argument — that DHA esterified at the middle glycerol position
survives pancreatic lipase as 2-monoglyceride and is better absorbed — is
biochemically real but clinically unproven. Dyerberg found that stereochemistry
did **not** influence bioavailability, and rTG, which is largely randomised
across positions, was the most bioavailable form ([R11](references.md)). Treat
sn-2 positioning as a plausible minor advantage, not a reason to pay a premium.

## Enteric coating and capsule shell

Enteric coating delays release until the intestine, which reduces fishy burping
and reflux. It does **not** meaningfully improve bioavailability, and it does not
fix a rancid oil. Fish-gelatin shells serve pescatarian and religious needs but
are more expensive and can release oil more slowly than bovine gelatin. Neither
is a quality marker.

## What to look for on the label

1. **Separate EPA and DHA milligrams**, not "total omega-3" or "fish oil 1,200 mg".
2. **Serving size** (often two softgels, not one).
3. **Chemical form**, if the brand charges a premium for rTG.
4. **Fish species or algal source.**
5. **A lot-level certification** (IFOS) or pharmacopoeial verification (USP), if
   the product claims one.
6. **An expiry date** and, for liquids, a nitrogen flush or antioxidant
   declaration.
