# Independent testing and certifications

Supplement certification is confusing because different programmes test
different things, and none of them tests whether the product works. This chapter
separates the claims.

## What each programme actually covers

| Programme | Potency vs label | Contaminants | Oxidation | GMP / facility | Banned substances | Public lot reports |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **USP Verified** | Yes | Yes (specified) | Monograph-dependent | Yes | No | No (programme-level) |
| **NSF Certified for Sport** | Yes | Yes | Not in stated scope | Yes | Yes (~290+ substances) | No |
| **IFOS** | Yes | Yes | **Yes** | No | No | **Yes, per lot** |
| **MSC** | No | No | No | No | No | Fishery only |
| **Friend of the Sea** | No | No | No | No | No | Sourcing only |
| **GOED membership** | No | No | No | No | No | No |

### USP Verified

Audits the facility against GMP, reviews quality documentation, tests against the
USP–NF monograph or equivalent for identity, potency, contaminants and
dissolution, and performs annual off-the-shelf surveillance
([R39](references.md)). It is a genuine quality programme. It says nothing about
clinical efficacy, and a USP mark does not mean the product contains the dose
that any trial used.

### NSF Certified for Sport

Builds on NSF/ANSI 173 certification and adds screening for roughly 290
substances banned by WADA and major sports organisations, plus label and
formulation review and facility inspection ([R40](references.md)). It is the
relevant certification for drug-tested athletes. Oxidation is not part of its
published scope.

### IFOS

The only widely used programme that tests **oxidation plus potency plus
contaminants on a lot-by-lot basis and publishes the results**, including
peroxide value, p-anisidine value and TOTOX ([R41](references.md)). That makes it
the most useful seal for a consumer worried about rancidity. Caveats:

- Certification is **lot-specific**; a five-star report for one lot does not
  guarantee the bottle you buy next year.
- IFOS is run by a commercial lab (Nutrasource), and brands pay for it.
- "IFOS 5-star" is a rating, not a regulatory grade, and "pharmaceutical grade"
  has no legal definition.

### MSC and Friend of the Sea

These certify **sourcing and traceability**, not composition. MSC certifies the
fishery and the chain of custody ([R42](references.md)); Friend of the Sea
certifies that the oil comes from approved fisheries or by-products, with
conditions on discards, endangered species and social accountability
([R43](references.md)). Both are meaningful for sustainability and neither says
anything about EPA+DHA content or oxidation.

### GOED membership

GOED is a trade association. Members sign an affidavit to comply with the
monograph and code of ethics, and GOED runs a small randomized testing programme
of member products (about 20-24 products a year). GOED itself states that its
member logo **is not a certification programme or quality seal**
([R23](references.md)). Membership is a weak signal, not a test result.

## What independent testing has found

The published record is mixed and worth reading in full
([`generated/quality-surveys.md`](generated/quality-surveys.md)):

- **Label accuracy:** US testing found a mean of 102% of labelled EPA+DHA, with
  59.5% of products at or above label ([R27](references.md)). A New Zealand study
  found a mean of only 68% and just 3 of 32 products meeting label
  ([R25](references.md)). A 2024 US study of 15 products found only 6 within ±20%
  for EPA and 10 for DHA. Quality is not uniform across markets or suppliers.
- **Oxidation:** 13% of unflavoured US products versus 68% of flavoured products
  exceeded TOTOX in one survey ([R29](references.md)). Unflavoured softgels from
  established brands usually pass.
- **Contaminants:** refined fish oils are consistently low in mercury; dioxins
  and PCBs are low but shark-liver oils are outliers ([R35](references.md)).
- **ConsumerLab** has reported that, in its 2023 testing, three products exceeded
  oxidation limits, sixteen passed, and none exceeded heavy-metal limits; product
  names are subscriber-only, so this review does not name them
  ([R27](references.md) discusses the same methodological issue).

## How to use certification when buying

1. **For freshness:** IFOS lot report, or a brand that publishes a recent COA.
2. **For general quality:** USP Verified or NSF Certified for Sport.
3. **For athletes:** NSF Certified for Sport.
4. **For sustainability:** MSC or Friend of the Sea.
5. **For nothing:** the words "pharmaceutical grade", "molecularly distilled" or
   "GOED member" on their own.

A product can be certified and still be poor value if it delivers 90 mg of
EPA+DHA per capsule at $4 per gram. Quality and value are different axes; the
[brand comparison](generated/brand-comparison.md) scores the second.
