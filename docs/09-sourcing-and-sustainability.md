# Sourcing, species and sustainability

## Where omega-3 comes from

Global fish oil production is **1.0-1.3 million tonnes a year** (about 1.30 Mt in
2024), containing roughly **160,000 tonnes of EPA+DHA** ([R105](references.md),
[R106](references.md)). That is a small pool of a nutrient that several industries
want.

The allocation matters more than any brand story:

| Use | Share of fish oil (2024) |
| --- | ---: |
| Aquaculture feed | **~64%** |
| Direct human consumption (supplements, functional food, pharma) | **~17%** |
| Other (pet food, biofuel, industrial) | **~20%** |

Fishmeal is even more concentrated: over 90% goes to aquaculture. In 2021 the
split was 73% aquaculture, 16% human, 11% other ([R104](references.md)).

Two further facts complicate the "fish oil is a waste product" narrative in both
directions:

- **About 54% of fish oil now comes from by-products and trimmings** rather than
  whole fish, up from a much smaller share two decades ago
  ([R104](references.md)). That is a genuine efficiency gain.
- **Roughly 18 million tonnes of wild fish are still reduced to meal and oil each
  year**, about 20% of global capture ([R104](references.md)).

## The forage-fish problem

Small pelagic fish — anchovy, sardine, mackerel, menhaden, sand eel — are the
base of the marine food web. Reducing them to feed farmed salmon and to fill
capsules is a nutrient-conversion problem: you feed omega-3 to a fish and recover
only part of it.

For Norwegian salmon, the fish-in-fish-out ratio for **oil** was about **1.8** in
2020 (1.5 in 2016), and only about **26% of the EPA+DHA fed to salmon is retained
in the edible fillet** ([R107](references.md), [R108](references.md)). Put
bluntly: feeding wild fish oil to farmed salmon loses about three-quarters of the
omega-3 before it reaches a plate. Direct human consumption of that oil is a more
efficient route to the nutrient, which is one argument for supplements and algal
oil.

## Peru and the anchoveta shock

Peru is the single most important source. Estimates of its share vary by metric:
**10-15% of global fish oil** (EUMOFA), **~20% of fishmeal and fish oil** (IFFO),
or **a quarter to a third of globally traded** meal and oil (FAO)
([R105](references.md)). Peruvian anchoveta is also highly sensitive to El Niño.

In 2026, a strong El Niño led Peru to close the first anchoveta season early with
only about 25% of the quota caught, and then to cancel the second season
entirely ([R112](references.md)). That is a material supply shock: it raises
prices, pressures smaller brands, and is a reminder that the omega-3 supply chain
is concentrated in one climate-sensitive fishery. It is also the strongest
practical argument for **algal oil** and for **certified by-product oils**.

## Krill

Antarctic krill is managed by CCAMLR with a precautionary catch limit of
**5.61 million tonnes** across Subareas 48.1-48.4 and a **620,000-tonne trigger**
that closes the fishery ([R109](references.md)). Recent seasons have hit the
trigger:

- **2023/24:** about 500,000 t, then a record.
- **2025:** **624,917 t**, a record, with 57% taken in Subarea 48.1; the fishery
  closed early in August.
- **2026:** the 620,000 t trigger was reached on 12 August, the second
  consecutive early closure.

Krill oil itself is a small fraction of the catch — estimates range from **under
1,000 tonnes** to about **2,700 tonnes** of oil a year, with the higher figure
possibly inflated by trade-data errors ([R110](references.md)).

The sustainability concern is not krill extinction; it is **localised competition
with predators**. Krill fishing overlaps spatially with penguin and whale
foraging, and 75% of catches near the South Orkneys have been taken within 80 km
of land during breeding season. The industry has adopted voluntary buffer zones
around colonies, but CCAMLR has not legislated them
([R109](references.md), [R110](references.md)).

MSC certification of the krill fishery is real and audited, but it certifies the
fishery management system, not the absence of local ecosystem pressure.

## Species and what they mean for the product

| Species / source | Oil characteristics | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Anchovy (*Engraulis ringens*) | High EPA+DHA, low contaminant load after refining | The backbone of the supplement supply; climate-sensitive |
| Sardine, mackerel, herring | High EPA+DHA | Common in "small fish" blends; good contaminant profile |
| Alaska pollock | Moderate EPA+DHA; by-product oil | MSC-certified fisheries; used by several premium brands |
| Cod liver | Moderate EPA+DHA plus **vitamins A and D** | Vitamin toxicity risk; not a generic fish oil |
| Tuna | High DHA; higher mercury in flesh, low in refined oil | Often blended |
| Shark liver | High squalene and alkylglycerols; **high POPs** | Recurring dioxin/PCB outlier; avoid unless tested |
| Krill | Phospholipid EPA+DHA, astaxanthin | Low concentration per capsule |
| Microalgae | DHA and/or EPA, no fish | Vegan; scalable; cost-competitive at the low end |

## Certification and what it covers

- **MSC** certifies the fishery and chain of custody. Hundreds of supplement
  products carry it, and it is the strongest widely available sustainability
  signal ([R42](references.md), [R111](references.md)).
- **Friend of the Sea** certifies sourcing, including by-products and social
  accountability, but is a narrower scheme ([R43](references.md)).
- **GOED membership** is not an environmental certification.

Neither MSC nor Friend of the Sea tests potency or oxidation. A product can be
sustainably sourced and rancid.

## Manufacturing and the environment

The steps that matter environmentally are rendering (energy and water intensive),
transesterification and molecular distillation (energy intensive), and packaging
(glass and plastic). Nitrogen flushing and opaque bottles improve shelf life,
which indirectly reduces waste. There is no credible evidence that rTG is worse
for the environment than EE; the difference is process cost, not resource
intensity at scale.

## Practical sustainability guidance

1. **Prefer algal oil** if you want to avoid wild-fish pressure entirely. It is
   the only source that scales without a fishery.
2. **Prefer certified by-product oils** (pollock, salmon trimmings) over
   whole-fish reduction oils.
3. **Avoid shark liver oil** — the highest contaminant risk and the least
   defensible sourcing.
4. **Do not assume "wild" or "Alaskan" means sustainable**; check the MSC or FOS
   claim and its scope.
5. **Take only the dose you need.** The most sustainable capsule is the one you
   do not take for a benefit that has not been shown.

The uncomfortable summary: the omega-3 supplement industry depends on a
climate-sensitive forage fishery that is mostly feeding other fish, and the
cheapest way to reduce its environmental footprint is to consume less of it,
from better sources.
